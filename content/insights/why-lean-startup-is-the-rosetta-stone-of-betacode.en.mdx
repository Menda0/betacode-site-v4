## What we learned building Wishmood

Wishmood was an on-demand delivery app for beaches, outdoor events and places traditional apps did not reach: food delivered to your sunbed, picnic or festival tent. The idea came from a real observation. Beachgoers wanted food, and nearby restaurants had no digital channel.

We built fast: app, restaurant onboarding, logistics and payments. But we learned that shipping is not the same as learning.

- We assumed year-round demand, and it was strongly seasonal.
- We built features before knowing whether restaurants could handle rush hours.
- We optimised the app before proving the unit economics worked.
- In interviews people loved the idea. Real usage told another story.

> "People want this" and "people pay enough for this to be a business" are very different claims.

## From founders to enablers

In 2016 we founded Betacode around a question: if lean methods helped us learn faster, could they help other companies do the same? Today it runs through everything we do: MVP development, technical consulting focused on what to build first, and Betacode Ventures, where we invest our team and let the market decide.

## What lean startup means in practice

1. Build: ship the smallest version that tests the riskiest assumption.
2. Measure: collect data from real users, not opinions from meetings.
3. Learn: decide whether to persevere, pivot or stop.

## How we apply it

Startups: define the riskiest assumption before coding, almost always "will anyone pay?". Launch with one core workflow, not a feature matrix, and talk to users every week.

Tech companies: validate new product lines with prototypes before committing a team for six months, and measure outcomes (retention, revenue, support tickets), not output.

Traditional companies: start with the business problem, not a technology wish list. Pilot in one department and keep the old systems running while the new one proves itself.

## The mistakes we keep seeing

- Stealth build: six months in isolation and a launch nobody asked for.
- Feature factory: shipping nonstop without checking whether usage or revenue moved.
- Perfect architecture: months of infrastructure before any user touches the product.
- Committee spec: 50 pages written by people who won’t use the software.
- Sunk cost: carrying on because "we have already invested so much".

## Why it still matters in 2026

AI writes code faster and cloud infrastructure appears in minutes. The cost of building has dropped. The cost of building the wrong thing has not, and may have risen, because you can now ship bad ideas faster than ever.

> What is the riskiest assumption? What is the smallest thing that tests it? What do the data say? Persevere or pivot? That is our Rosetta Stone.
