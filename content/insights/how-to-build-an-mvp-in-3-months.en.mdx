## Why three months

- Preserves runway: a time limit keeps burn under control.
- Speeds up learning: real users tell you early whether to persevere, pivot or stop.
- Gives you an edge over those still wireframing in month six.
- Changes investor conversations: "we launched six weeks ago, here are the numbers".
- Kills scope creep: every feature has to earn its place.

## \1. Start with a written plan

Short and shared: the problem, the core user journey, the technical approach, a build list and a don’t-build list, success metrics and who decides what. At Betacode we co-create it with the founder in the first two weeks.

## \2. Identify the essentials

- If removing a feature makes the product useless, build it.
- If it only makes it less convenient, defer it.
- If it only matters at scale, wait for scale.
- If no user asked for it, don’t build it.

<Note title="Example: Coach ID">
The MVP focused on one thing: letting coaches plan and run weekly training. Payments, the AI assistant, the admin dashboard and the drill editor came after the core loop was validated.
</Note>

## \3. Two-week milestones

1. Weeks 1–2: plan locked, architecture set, environment ready.
2. Weeks 3–4: data models and endpoints for the core flow.
3. Weeks 5–6: frontend wired to backend, full flow in staging.
4. Weeks 7–8: internal testing and critical bug fixes.
5. Weeks 9–10: production, monitoring and first users.
6. Weeks 11–12: feedback, fixes and the persevere-or-pivot decision.

Weekly demos of working software (not slides), blockers escalated after a day and a founder who is always reachable. Waiting three days for a product decision kills momentum.

## \4. AI as an accelerator

AI doesn’t replace the team, but it compresses repetitive work: scaffolding, CRUD endpoints, schemas, tests and docs. People still own architecture, experience and production debugging. The time saved becomes extra polish or an on-time launch.

## What kills a three-month timeline

- "While we’re at it…": every addition costs weeks.
- Perfectionism in design, architecture or naming.
- Building in stealth, without talking to users.
- The wrong team: disappearing freelancers or hourly agencies.
- No metrics, or defending the product instead of listening.

> Three months is enough to build something real, launch it and learn whether the idea has traction. The deadline is the method.
